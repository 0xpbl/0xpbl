## 🌪️ The wind that scrapes

In the universe of **Pó-Blanc**, the wind does not blow.

It scrapes.

It moves across the dunes like sandpaper on old bone, raises clouds of white dust, enters the lungs of cities and leaves everyone with the permanent impression that the whole world has just emerged from a botched renovation.

There, the seas are not made of water.

They are made of crushed mineral salt, dry foam, chalky mist and fine residue of ancient mountains ground down by engines that no longer exist. Ships do not float: they creak. Ports do not welcome: they charge. And the moons, when they appear, look like effervescent tablets dissolving in the sky.

It was in this place of pale rust, harsh wind and zero elegance that **Paulo Pirata** appeared.

---

## 👤 Paulo Pirata

Sixty-seven years old.

A bad leg.

The lopsided gait of someone who always seems to be stepping off a deck in a storm, even when standing still.

A nose constantly dirty with a strip of white dust that never comes off — no one knows if it’s sand from Pó-Blanc, explosion residue, road dust, ash from a burnt amplifier or just the universe signing him with its own mineral mould.

And yet: **imposing**.

Not handsome.

Not noble.

Not respectable.

Imposing.

Because Paulo Pirata had that rare kind of presence that comes not from beauty or morals, but from the clear sense that he had already survived things that would kill entire peoples and had not even improved his mood.

He was a pirate.

He was a rocker.

He was a biker.

Not because he chose three styles.

But because in Pó-Blanc those three curses had ended up becoming the same profession.

There, the pirate did not board galleons. He raided convoys of liturgical gasoline, freighters of holy copper guitar strings, monasteries on wheels, funeral trains and caravans of smugglers carrying luxury, wine, gunpowder, car batteries, forbidden records and sauce recipes that would justify war.

---

## 🏍️ The Widow of Lime

His “ship” was a monstrous motorcycle called **The Widow of Lime** (A Viúva de Cal).

It had handlebars wide as blasphemy, a fuel tank patched with funeral plaques, an exhaust like a church organ and a black pirate sail fastened behind the seat, flapping in the dry wind as if mocking the very notion of aerodynamics.

When Paulo revved, the sound did not resemble an engine.

It resembled a satanic sermon delivered inside a metal barrel thrown down a flight of stairs.

Children ran indoors.

The elderly closed their windows.

Dogs barked at God.

And the **dust priests** — thin men in grey robes who preached humility, silence and moderate carburation — made the sign of the piston and said the same thing:

> *"There goes that wretched cripple again."*

But in Pó-Blanc no one became a legend for riding a bike and talking tough.

You became a legend by having **ballads**.

And Paulo Pirata had many.

Not songs about him.

Though he had those too, and almost all were bad.

No.

The ballads of Paulo Pirata were **episodes sung** in dust taverns, sacrilegious workshops, failed neon alleys and fuel stations that also served soup, ammunition, confession and vaccine against spiritual rust.

## 🔦 The Ballad of the Crooked Lighthouse

They say that on the dry coast of **Brancamar** there stood a lighthouse built from the spine of an ancient mineral leviathan. It guided convoys through ash storms and charged toll in fuel and promises.

One year, the lighthouse began to lie.

Instead of saving travellers, it drew them to cliffs of electric dust, where looters collected cargo and bones with corporate efficiency.

For months, no one dared climb to the lamp chamber, because the place was guarded by **signal monks**, men without eyebrows who considered the blinking of lights a form of theology.

Paulo Pirata climbed.

Not for heroism.

Not for justice.

He climbed because a convoy carrying volcanic molasses whisky had vanished there, and Paulo considered that a metaphysical crime.

He reached the top limping, spitting dust, with an empty bottle on his belt and a serrated machete called **Refrain**.

There he found the secret: no demon, no imperial conspiracy, no malign intelligence.

Just a bored lighthouse operator, blind from dust, deeply incompetent and listening to terrible music at criminal volume.

Paulo did not give a speech.

He did not reflect.

He did not put on the face of a cheap novel protagonist.

He just ripped the sound system off the wall, threw the operator down the stairs and realigned the lamp with a kick.

On the way back, he set the lighthouse ledger on fire, stole three barrels of rescued whisky and said the phrase that became a tavern refrain for decades:

> *"Lying with light is worse than stealing in the dark."*

## 🎰 The Ballad of the Drowned Casino of Gesso-Rei

The second ballad, sung more among mechanics and violent widows, was **The Ballad of the Drowned Casino of Gesso-Rei**.

In the heart of the white desert there was an underground casino carved into petrified salt, where gang bosses, nitrate magnates and dust captains gambled gold teeth, territories and childhood memories.

Paulo was invited once.

The invitation said:

```txt
CASUAL WEAR, LIGHT WEAPON, GRUDGE OPTIONAL.
```

He showed up in dirty boots, cracked leather jacket, a patch sewn on the back reading **"DON'T HONK AT THE ABYSS"**, and brought enough grudge to fuel the house for a quarter.

He sat at the main table, lost at cards, won on threat and, after three rounds, realised the casino was cheating with dice made from the bones of defaulters.

That he could respect.

What irritated him was the band’s repertoire.

A lounge band playing domesticated rock, no grit, no mistake, no risk, no soul — music for combed cowards.

Paulo rose in the middle of the final hand, dragged his bad leg like someone carrying bad omens in his hip, took the resident musician’s bass and played so loud, so dirty, so violently beyond good company that the ceiling began to crack.

Tables overturned.

Chandeliers fell.

The manager lost two teeth and his belief in civilisation.

When the subsurface police arrived, they found only rubble, melted chips, a traumatised albino alligator and, painted on the wall with spilled drink and soot:

> *"IF THE SOUND IS TOO CLEAN, THE CRIME HAS ALREADY BEGUN."*

## 💔 The Ballad of the Seven Ex-Girlfriends of Chrome

Then came **The Ballad of the Seven Ex-Girlfriends of Chrome**.

This one is harder to separate from legend, because every town tells a different version and they all sound like lies, which in Pó-Blanc is usually a strong sign of truth.

In this story, Paulo was said to have been pursued by seven mechanical women — singers, smugglers, duelists or priestesses — no one agrees — with whom he had been involved at different times and left owing emotional, material or explosive debts.

One wanted back a ring made from a carburettor spring.

Another wanted revenge for a serenade that ended in fire.

Another wanted the map to a cave where the echo answered with personalised insults.

Another simply wanted to kill him because “it was about time”.

For seven nights he crossed Pó-Blanc with the Widow of Lime coughing fire, while the seven hunted him on smaller, faster bikes ridden with far greater skill.

Paulo escaped them all not by genius, but because the universe, for some reason, had the bad habit of rewarding his animal persistence.

On the last night, cornered in a salt amphitheatre, Paulo did what any sensible man would never do:

he climbed onto a collapsing stage, wired six amplifiers in series, shouted **"IF YOU'RE GONNA KILL ME, AT LEAST TUNE FIRST"** and began to play a riff so obscene, so disrespectful, so emotionally unresolved that the seven stopped.

Not from forgiveness.

Not from love.

From technical recognition.

He was still a scoundrel.

But he played like someone who had survived his own funeral several times.

They say no one died that night.

They say three went back with him.

They say one stole his wallet in the middle of the solo.

All three versions are compatible.

## ⛪ The Ballad of the Church of the Last Chord

The most famous of all, however, is **The Ballad of the Church of the Last Chord**.

That one turned Paulo Pirata from folk outlaw into **respected calamity**.

On the plateau of **Chalk Mercy** (Misericórdia Calcária) there was a church built around an **ancestral amplifier**, a colossal liturgical machine that, according to the devout, contained the first chord ever played in Pó-Blanc, still reverberating inside the metal like a sacred plague.

The faithful kept that chord as a relic.

No one could hear it.

No one could play it.

No one could amplify it.

It existed only as promise.

And promise, as always, was worth more than reality.

Paulo Pirata heard about it at a roadside stall, drank three glasses of filtered sweet gasoline, stared at the informant with his nose white with dust, and said:

> *"If there's a locked sound, there's a cowardly owner."*

Three days later, he showed up at the church.

Bike roaring.

Black flag whipping the air.

Bad leg.

Bad eye.

Worse mood.

The **priests of the Last Chord** tried to stop him with doctrine, protocol, controlled-feedback novenas and a very poorly organised human chain.

Paulo passed them all.

Not running.

Limping.

Slowly.

With that odious stride of a man who has already decided to do something stupid and now only needs to find the right door.

He entered the central sanctuary.

Saw the ancestral amplifier, black, enormous, covered in mineral salt and wires thick as an old serpent.

And laughed.

> *"Is that all?"*

That phrase, in Pó-Blanc, has killed more people than plague.

Paulo switched on the machine.

The first chord came out.

It was not music.

It was a **spiritual collapse**.

The church walls trembled.

The saints lost their faith.

The pews exploded into devout splinters.

The bells rang by themselves in panic.

Across the whole village, glass burst, dentures vibrated, graves opened half a centimetre and three weddings were cancelled by instant revelation of moral incompatibility.

Paulo listened to it, standing still, covered in white dust and bad light, as if hearing the universe finally admit it had always wanted to be loud.

Then he switched it off.

Turned his back.

And said to the priests lying on the floor:

> *"You were praying to an amplifier."*

He left without apologising, without looking back and without paying for the damage.

From then on, the ballads changed tone.

Before, Paulo Pirata had been an outlaw as elegant as possible for a dusty, limping man.

After the Church of the Last Chord, he became something else.

He became a **road figure**.

A **mechanical apparition**.

A **saint of the cursed workshop**.

A **plague of the border post**.

The mothers of Pó-Blanc threatened their children:

> *"Sleep or Paulo Pirata will come and tune your cradle in E minor."*

Bar owners took out insurance.

Convoys doubled their guard when they heard an engine in the distance.

And mediocre musicians, the more prudent ones, retired early.

## 📖 Minor ballads

There were other, lesser ballads.

**The Robbery of the Fossil-Bridge**, when he held up an entire toll using only aggressive charisma and a bicycle chain.

**The Duel of the Three Gears**, when he beat an armoured colonel because the colonel’s bike was too pretty for a real road.

**The Night He Drank with the Neon Gravediggers**, of which no one remembers the beginning or the end, only the undisputed fact that six graves woke up with autographs.

---

## 👃 The Ballad of the White Nose

And the terrible **Ballad of the White Nose**, which the old tell in a low voice: that the dirt on his face was not ordinary dirt. It would be residual dust from the dead zones between cities, a mineral that only sticks to men already half unhinged from reality. They say the whiter the mark on his nose, the closer Paulo was to doing something unforgettable, stupid or irreversible.

He never explained.

Never denied.

Never confirmed.

He would just wipe it with the back of his hand, glare at anyone who noticed and say:

> *"Mind your own snout."*

---

## 🏴‍☠️ That was Paulo Pirata

That was **Paulo Pirata**.

Sixty-seven years.

Pirate of the dry roads.

Rocker by moral deformation.

Biker by faith in the engine.

Lame as if the very ground had tried to stop him and failed by a hair.

In Pó-Blanc, no king commanded further than the dust, and no priest shouted louder than the exhaust of the Widow of Lime.

And among dunes of lime, ports of salt and cities where the wind left everything tasting of bone, the ballads kept being sung.

Not always accurate.

Always exaggerated.

As every good legend must be.

Because in decent universes, men like Paulo Pirata die anonymous on some roadside.

But Pó-Blanc was never a decent universe.

It was the kind of place that took an old, lame, dirty, insolent man too loud for society…

…and turned that into **mythology**.
